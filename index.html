About the numbers

Consumer prices are national CPI as collected by the World Bank, not the harmonised HICP — the two differ by a point or more over a decade. Pay is the OECD's average annual wage per full-time equivalent employee, in nominal national currency, so it is an average and not a typical worker's pay. Housing is the residential property price index for existing and new dwellings — purchase prices, not rents.

The mortgage line prices the same house at today's rates: it multiplies the change in house prices by the change in the monthly annuity payment, assuming an unchanged loan-to-value ratio and a 25-year repayment mortgage at the country's typical rate for new housing loans. Deposit and fees scale with the price; tax relief, term changes and fixation periods are not modelled.

Still missing: any pay or house price figure for Cyprus, which appears in neither OECD dataset, and a measured mortgage rate for the ten countries covered by neither the ECB’s euro-area series nor a national one, which keep estimates.

Why these numbers and not others

National CPI rather than HICP. Eurostat's harmonised index is the better basis for comparing European countries with each other, and it is what this site should use. It is not what it uses. The World Bank's national CPI was reachable, covers all 24 countries including the US and Australia in one consistent series, and goes back to 1960, where HICP starts in 1996 and excludes the US entirely. The cost of that choice is real: national CPI and HICP differ by more than a point over a decade for some countries, and they treat owner-occupied housing differently. The figures come from the World Bank's own bulk download, committed in the repository: an earlier build read a GitHub mirror of it that stopped a year short and carried a wrong Romanian value for 2024, which taking the file direct fixed.

Nominal everywhere, never real. Every series here is in cash of the day. Real series — inflation-adjusted pay, real house prices — are published for all of these and would be wrong to use, because this site's whole job is to divide one series by another. A real wage already has consumer prices divided out of it; comparing it against consumer prices would count inflation twice and understate the gap. The same goes for the price-to-income and price-to-rent ratios OECD publishes alongside its house price index.

Average annual wages rather than a labour cost index. Eurostat's labour cost index is current and harmonised, but it measures what an hour of labour costs an employer, including social contributions and other non-wage costs. OECD's average annual wages is closer to what appears on a payslip. It is still an average per full-time equivalent employee, so it says nothing about how pay is spread, which is why the minimum wage is charted beside it. In most of Europe the floor rose faster than the average, so the lowest paid did better than the average line suggests; in the United States it did the opposite.

The minimum wage from the same publisher as the average. The point of that chart is the floor measured against the average, so the two lines have to be built the same way. They now are: both come from the OECD’s Earnings database, in national currency at current prices, and the OECD publishes the year directly rather than a monthly rate to multiply by twelve. Eurostat supplied all of them until this file arrived and still supplies Cyprus, which is not an OECD member. The two mostly agree — for eleven of the sixteen countries both cover, to the rounding — but not always, and where they differ it is because a country sets an hourly or monthly floor and turning that into a year needs an assumption about normal weekly hours. For the Netherlands in 2025 that assumption is worth 8.4%: €28,880 against €26,634. Whichever publisher drew a country’s line is named under the chart. What no annual figure can show is an hourly floor, and all of them assume full-time work all year.

National house price indices for the US and UK. OECD publishes house prices for both, and this site ignores those in favour of Case-Shiller and Nationwide, on the grounds that a country's own index beats a cross-country compilation of it. That choice moves the answer: for 2016 to 2025 OECD's US series says +90.6% where Case-Shiller says +83.5%. Seven points, from nothing but the choice of index.

Gross pay. Tax and social contributions are not modelled anywhere. Tax wedges changed over these decades and modelling 23 tax systems across sixty years is a different project. Every salary figure on this site is therefore before deductions, including the mortgage-qualifying figure, which is how lenders quote it anyway.

One mortgage rate for the whole euro area. The ECB series used here is the euro-area aggregate, so thirteen countries share one rate and the real differences between them are missing rather than measured. Per-country rates exist in the same ECB dataset and would be a straightforward improvement. Australia is the one country outside the euro area with a rate of its own — the RBA’s discounted variable housing rate, which is what the banks advertise rather than what borrowers end up paying, and runs about half a point above the rate on new loans. The other ten countries still have estimates, and the mortgage figures are marked accordingly.
